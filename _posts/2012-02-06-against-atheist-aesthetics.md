# Against Atheist Aesthetics

Below is an extract from an academic article of mine still in the works (and still being edited) that explains the problems of knowledge for Analytical philosophy, especially that borrowed and utilized by atheists. The following is an argument that shows how most contemporary atheists views of knowledge cannot adequately account for the experience of beauty and art. Enjoy.
_______________________________
Now we move on to the Rationalists approach defended by Abū ‘l-Hasan b. Al-Hasan Ibn al-Haytham al-Baṣrī al-Miṣrī, the late Fatimid physicist, mathematician and astronomer who advanced the field of optics in his famous treatise, Kitāb al-Manāẓir (The Book of Optics)1. Though the present analysis of Islamic thinkers is primarily taken from the work of Valerie Gonzalez2 we disagree with her assessment that “Ibn Haytham is the one and only medieval scholar to precede modern thinkers in their attempt to isolate the question of aesthetics from the divine sphere and to consider it in terms of a strictly human ontology”3 and that he was the one to introduce a phenomenological method by which to conceptualize beauty4. Even within her own research she applies the same humanistic ontological distinction to Ibn Ḥazm’s theory5 (which will be discussed at a later point) and fails to distinguish the rationalistic from the phenomenological methods formally created by Edmund Husserl6. Perhaps this has to do with her definition of what constitutes as phenomenology: “Phenomenological works, in particular, allow a deeper grasp of the primordial meaning of forms and intelligibles that, in the framework of the visual arts, help to reveal the essence of being of an object as it appears to the sight, providing an invaluable source of knowledge”7.
Under this definition she confines phenomenology primarily to sense experience, which in fact is only a partial understanding of the method. Not only is the method concerned with what appears to us, but also the structure of the experience and its context8. In other words, it is also about how we interpret the appearances in a meaningful way and what meaning is derived.
Ibn al-Haytham is primarily concerned with the objects of our experience and the features that illicit an “aesthetic response” (identification of beauty)9 from our intuitive cognitive abilities, rather than the structure of those experiences and what they mean:
“Indeed,in Ibn al-Haytham’s work, as Puerta Vìlchez rightly says, aesthetic appreciation appears entirely circumscribed within the intuitive and sensitive cognitive field, and does not go through the demonstration of logic. It is an experience of knowledge free from subordination to ethics, logic, and the religious reason to which medieval authors generally confine it.”10
As such, Ibn al-Haytham cannot authentically be considered a Phenomenologist, but an individual who applies a more objectively scientific method to understanding beauty, therefore falling more in line with a purely Rationalists mode of thinking. For Ibn al-Haytham, beauty is within the objects themselves, indeed, as Gonzalez explains:
“Immediately, Ibn al-Haytham recognizes beauty and ugliness as objective and visible facts which all objects or corporeal beings (ajsān) display in varying degrees…These properties or concepts are gathered into a basic list containing exactly twenty-two notions including light, colour, form, volume and so on.”11
These specific properties are seen as beautiful in isolation, but they are also considered to have a measurable quality when combined with other properties12, which Gonzalez calls Ibn al-Haytham’s “modulator principle”13. These properties of beauty are seen by Ibn al-Haytham as being objectively discerned through a sort of universally shared normative judgment14 (through cognition) in that all human beings, gradually from birth, are able to grasp it once they have matured:
[T]he beauty that clearly exists in all things must be grasped by the individual in its full complexity, variation and subtlety, as and when his perceptive ability matures, improves and sharpens with practice and use, starting from the elementary experience of the child…Thus it concerns a complex process of visual perception that appeals to the combination of sensory grasp with a form of understanding derived from the imaginative faculties and other modes of intuitive or pre-logical knowledge.15
A number of questions are raised from this analysis. First, how does Ibn al-Haytham deal with differences in tastes (aesthetic judgment)? Certainly people appreciate things in different ways and as having varying degrees of value. While Ibn al-Haytham may suggest that these are merely different degrees of understanding and that there is in fact an objective beauty that all people can recognize and agree upon once they observe and grasp the properties of the object, the mere fact that he confines these properties to material forms makes his theory implausible. If what is beautiful in the object is merely the physical qualities it possesses (whether in isolation or combined) then this reduces other features of our aesthetic experience that bring about the same, if not transcend, degrees of pleasure and displeasure. For instance, Ibn al-Haytham would have difficulty blaming a lack of aesthetic understanding or proper cognition on an individual who sees more value in a work of art based on the context of an opposing philosophical framework by which to judge beauty. Here, he would have to argue against the opposing theory itself, but he would be limited to the mere physical properties of the object, which in and of themselves are merely descriptive attributes and not prescriptive. In appealing to normative cognition, he would have to explain why we can only universally ascertain descriptive features, while ignoring the fact that people see beauty in the meaning, symbolism and background of objects as well. Take for instance a picture of two perfectly straight lines placed next to one another. They may elicit absolutely no pleasure or displeasure in the observer until the individual recognizes that one of the lines was artificially produced digitally, whereas the other was produced by hand. In this realization the value of the latter may supersede the former by the recognition of the artist’s steady hand and ability to do what most cannot. A more complex example may be seen in the judgment of great works of fine art. While the properties of the painting may draw our attention, our judgment of its beauty may vary depending not on our understanding of its form, but of what it symbolizes or reminds us of, as in a positive or negative personal experience. Both these states can also be reflected in images of sunsets or mountain peaks. Our pleasure and displeasure with objects at this level cannot be measured in any way. It becomes even more problematic when we introduce the concept of truth and falsehood. If something gives us a realization of truth or falsehood, or if something conceptualizes that which is true or false, our appreciation of it can drastically change; we may appeal to its specific properties and forms, but the reality in which it conveys may satisfy or dissatisfy us, especially if our initial perception of it was inaccurate. We may see the physical beauty of a human form, but once we understand that the form ‘tricks’ us in assuming it is real (as in the case of a manikin) and we realize the illusion, we lose our appreciation of that object as we expected a different ‘intention’ of the appearance. In a deeper sense, when we think we’ve drawn a valid conclusion from something in what it is trying to convey, such as in terms of a parable, we gain pleasure from our ability to understand the truth that it has revealed to us. In no way does Ibn al-Haytham’s theory touch on these genuine experiences of what is considered beautiful, as he narrows his focus only on the form that the object possesses. Perhaps his contemporary, Monroe Beardsley, may be able to assist us with these dilemmas.
The late American Analytical philosopher, educated at Yale University and professor at numerous prestigious institutions16, was well known for his work in aesthetic philosophy and produced a number of books, most notably Aesthetics: Problems in the Philosophy of Criticism (1958; reissued with a postscript, 1981), which is considered to this day as “the first systematic, well-argued, and critically informed philosophy of art in the analytic tradition”17. Like Ibn al-Haytham, he assigns beauty primarily to the objects themselves, however he develops a much more elaborate and comprehensive theory of assessing aesthetic quality on the basis of art criticism, emphasizing properties that prompt our aesthetic interpretations, and not just things we’re attracted to. In a way then, his approach is more phenomenological than his predecessor though he is still seeped in the Rationalists tradition.
He begins by defining aesthetic objects, which he sees the same as works of arts as “either an arrangement of conditions intended to be capable of affording an experience with marked aesthetic character or (incidentally) an arrangement belonging to a class or type of arrangements that is typically intended to have this capacity”18. Beardsley also places aesthetic objects within a different category from every day things in that he distinguishes “between physical objects and perceptual objects. In speaking of a thing being six feet by six feet in size and at rest, we’re speaking of a physical object; in speaking of a thing being dynamic and frightening, we’re speaking of a perceptual object”19. An aesthetic object can both be a physical object and a perceptual object (or merely the latter, in the sense of an abstract concept coming to mind, but it still retains reference in the physical), but it is different in that it offers a presentation which produces the experience: “[W]hat the aesthetic object must be, is the production. Thus the primary object of critical attention is the production of an artifact, and the basic job of the critic is to describe, interpret, and evaluate such productions”20. It must be noted that the experience is completely the work of the object for Beardsley, while the observer plays a passive role. In other words, not only is the aesthetic object presenting its form to us, but in its autonomy it literally gives us its meaning and the evaluation we have towards it21. Immediately his theory differs from Ibn al-Haytham’s in that he includes within aesthetic objects properties other than what physical form is conventionally understood as (lighting, color, height, width etc.); physical things are also ultimately perceptual (meaningful statements about the object). The understanding of the aesthetic therefore becomes entirely objective. This may seem strange at first to refer to perceptual things as having some physical substance, but he makes his point by suggesting that all perceptual activity have physical reference points. Take for instance his understanding of internal and external evidences that allows us to understand and appreciate an aesthetic object. “Internal evidence is evidence from direct inspection of the object”22, meaning there is something within the object that allows us to immediately appreciate it, such as the conventionally understood physical forms of lighting and organizational quality, whereas, “[e]xternal evidence is evidence from the psychological and social background of the object, from which we may infer something about the object itself”23 , such as the cultural context or time period in which the object was created. Interestingly, since it is the object itself that is informing us about what it is and what it means, external evidence merely seems a subset of internal evidence and is only distinguished by a lack of immediacy in its realization. For if an individual already knows the external features that molded the object, does not the understanding of the object therefore become immediate?
Further, Beardsley’s explanation on how we criticize an aesthetic object is telling:
Critical statements are of three kinds, Beardsley thinks: descriptive, interpretative, and evaluative. The first concerns non-normative properties of works of art that are simply in it, in some sense, and are available, at least in principle, to anyone of normal eyes and ears if sufficiently sensitive, attentive, and experienced. ‘There is a small red patch in the upper right-hand corner of the painting’ is a descriptive statement, but so is ‘Haydn’s 23rd Symphony abounds in dynamic tension.’ The philosophical problems that descriptive statements give rise to involve the concept of form, Beardsley thinks. Interpretative statements are also non-normative, but concern the ‘meaning’ of a work of art, with ‘meaning’ here referring to a semantic relation, or at least a purported semantic relation, between the work and something outside it. ‘That’s a picture of Notary Sojac’ is an interpretative statement, as are ‘That’s a picture of a unicorn,’ ‘The passage refers to Brutus’s betrayal of Caesar,’ and ‘The thesis of Macbeth is exceedingly simple: Thou shalt not kill.’ Last, critical evaluations are normative judgments that basically say that a work of art is good or bad, or how good or bad it is.24
Beardsley’s conception of the interpretative element of criticism is what is most explicit in grounding his aesthetics purely within objects themselves in that ‘meaning’ is ultimately a semantic relation with other physical things. Even our understanding of a poem ultimately reflects back to physical objects in that our interpretation of it is merely a semantical relation of its own semantical relations. What is more interesting is that he considers both the descriptive and interpretative aspects of criticism as non-normative, meaning, that we can objectively understand the work of art, but that it presents no properties of what ought to be the case. Essentially, when we describe or interpret an aesthetic object, what we are doing is merely pointing out features that are not necessary to the objects evaluative quality. When we say that an object is “red and looks like a sports car”, all we’re doing is noticing attributes that can literally be gathered from several physically objective reference points. By increasing the number of references, Beardsley therefore overcomes the problem of different understandings of aesthetic objects by making them all equally valid 25.
Does Beardsley’s more comprehensive approach solve Ibn al-Haytham’s dilemmas? This appears to be the case as it grants more properties to aesthetic objects than the mere forms they possess by incorporating his notion of interpretation that is still genuinely grounded in the objects themselves. Further, Beardsley appears to resolve the issue of difference in tastes by acknowledging various descriptive and interpretative understandings of aesthetic objects through a multitude of objectively perceived reference points while still holding to normative judgments of their presentations. In regards to aesthetic truth, he doesn’t seem interested in the problem or consider it troubling at all, as it would merely fall within the same interpretative function of criticism.
Beardsley’s theory is not without flaws, however. By trying to ground aesthetic understanding in the object themselves, he actually undermines his very goal. For if aesthetic judgment is normative – the production of aesthetic objects can be factually observed as good or bad – then we cannot consider the descriptive or interpretative properties of said objects as non-normative. Let’s take the previous example of the object that is “red and looks like a sports car”. This is just one of many descriptive-interpretations that an aesthetic object may objectively possess. In fact, it may also be interpreted as “a blood red image symbolizing the decadence of our society and the dangers of progressing too quickly”. To Beardsley, this is equally a valid interpretation, but can it be said to be equally evaluative? Both of these examples could be combined without contradiction, but what if the interpretations do contradict? How does one say that something is actually good or bad if one objective interpretation disagrees with the other? Following the Laws of Identity and Non-contradiction, both interpretations cannot possibly be correct at the same time, so to suggest that a normative statement of evaluation can be made is simply incoherent because we would be judging two entirely different things and not the aesthetic object itself26! Beardsley may respond by suggesting in fact that one of these interpretations is simply false27 and fails to account for the appropriate reference points through an accurate analysis of both the internal and external properties that the object possesses. It is simply impossible, according to Beardsley, for an aesthetic object to give incompatible presentations, but then how does he justify claiming that interpretations are non-normative? Here, he could appeal to the various levels of understanding that people possess by suggesting that certain individuals simply know more than others about what they are perceiving. This would still not save Beardsley from our objection, however given that the knowledge of the aesthetic object would ultimately have to rest in how much it itself reveals to its observer. Beardsley does not account for the fact that the vagueness of the presentation, and not merely the interpreters’ lack of knowledge, may account for incompatible interpretations and yet still illicit normative evaluations. Let us bring up a hypothetical example: Imagine two men visit a contemporary art gallery and both observe a marble statue sculpted in the figure of a woman who has a particular expression on her face. Both men immediately evaluate the artwork as ‘good’ after examining the available internal and external evidences, but they form polar opposite interpretations. The first man perceives his wife within the object and her expression as the same right before they had their first kiss. The second man interprets a younger version of his mother and her expression as one after she felt proud of her son’s achievements. After evaluation, they share their experiences with one another. Both men may agree that for each of them, the interpretation is different and they may even concede that both may be valid relative to their own insights, but neither of them, according to Beardsley, could say that both interpretations are correct, since the first man’s wife is not identical to the second man’s mother (or so we hope!). Here, he cannot account for how two contradictory interpretations can simultaneously allow for the same aesthetic object to be normatively evaluated as ‘good’.
Beardsley may counter our hypothetical in three ways: a) by rejecting the normative evaluation of both these men on the basis of a lack of sufficient references and thereby rendering the perceived object unable to be defined as having aesthetic properties, b) by rejecting one interpretation as illusionary and therefore incorrect or c) by rejecting the hypothetical example as insufficient.
Firstly, if Beardsley rejects the normative evaluation, suggesting that the object can either be considered ‘good’ or ‘bad’ on the basis of a lack of sufficient references, then he must consequently believe that aesthetic objects with no concrete interpretations cannot be understood as being essentially aesthetic, meaning that the observer would have to remain neutral in determining if an object really has aesthetic properties. The problem with this is that people, as the two men in our example, do have aesthetic experiences despite their differing interpretations due to the vagueness of the object, for if they didn’t, they would make no evaluation to begin with.
Secondly, Beardsley may counter by suggesting that one of these men must be evaluating based on a false interpretation, therefore rendering his aesthetic experience as illusionary. If this is the case, however, where is the illusionary experience coming from if not the man’s perception of the object itself? Since an aesthetic experience is an experience of the aesthetic properties of an object, why is it that something like a marble statue of a woman can even allow the man to interpret from it other than what it really is? Since all interpretations ultimately are references to other objects by semantical relations, this would be like saying that those other physical references don’t actually exist for the object being perceived, raising the question: what then we are actually evaluating? Seeing as Beardsley confines himself to placing aesthetic properties in the objects themselves, he cannot blame the observer or point to the semantical relations as creating this illusion, since it is the work of art itself that is creating the experience. Even to say that a lack of knowledge of all the properties of the object are what are creating this illusion, it is insufficient because the object still appears to function aesthetically. This calls into question whether a correct interpretation is necessary for an object to grant an aesthetic experience. Based on the above hypothetical, it would not, thus suggesting that what makes an aesthetic experience what it is, is not exclusively within the objects themselves.
Finally, Beardsley may simply object to the hypothetical by insinuating that under his theory, neither men would come to such interpretative and evaluative conclusions. Ignoring for a moment the problem with the fact that both men do in fact have evaluative experiences despite differing interpretations and that illusions can authentically be considered an aesthetic experience – posing an issue for the objective understanding of aesthetic properties themselves – let us grant the possibility that Beardsley is correct. Let us say that if both men came upon the statue and through analyzing the internal and external evidences available with the same competency, they both could not adequately interpret the object and only generally assumed it to be ‘about a woman’. In other words, they both evaluate the object as ‘good’ while simultaneously coming to very similar understandings about its possible interpretation. Here, we put the final nail in the coffin for Beardlsey’s aesthetics by introducing the fact that, based on his own theory, both men must therefore intend for the object to have meaning which to derive before attempting to adequately interpret it. Before an object can be criticized, there first must be an intent to criticize through the assessment of the object’s aesthetic properties. In other words, the observer does not always act passively when initially approaching a work of art, rather, they actively engage the object with the intention of it offering them an aesthetic experience. If this were not the case, then the object would act autonomously in its presentation of meaning without us having to search for it. This proves fatal for Beardlsey’s theory due to the sheer fact that he denies the intentionality of the artist as an aesthetic property: “We must distinguish between the aesthetic object and the intention in the mind of its creator…”28 . This is consistent with his perspective, because if intentions were counted as a feature of aesthetic objects, then meaning would no longer rest autonomously within them, but their authors. What he does not seem to recognize is that the intentions of the observer may also play a role in how the object is perceived, especially if they adopt a specific paradigm, like Beardsley’s.
It stands to reason then that neither Ibn al-Hayham or Monroe Beardsley are able to adequately objectify beauty outside of the subjective. As the Idealists school, the Rationalists place beauty and our understanding of the concept outside the reach of genuine human experience, thus rendering it incoherent.
