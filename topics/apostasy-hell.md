There is exactly ONE recovered prose essay devoted to apostasy law ('My Views on the Punishment for Apostasy', 2,329 words, 2020), one prose essay on hell and divine justice ('The Fire of God's Mercy', catalogued under the slug 'gods-mercy-without-eternal-punishment', 3,231 words, 2018), and everything else is machine transcripts. FOUR of the seven seed titles are title-keyword false friends and contribute nothing to this cluster (see SEED TITLES EXCLUDED). The English-language essays 'When Religion Becomes Confusing' and 'An Alternate Reality' and 'Two Islands' survive under DIFFERENT files and different titles, which is why a title-only search misses them. The one genuinely rich, quotable source on the afterlife is the 2018 Ohio State MSA lecture transcript, not a written work. There is NO full scholarly treatment of hell, divine justice or theodicy anywhere in the archive: all twenty records in _papers/ are catalogue stubs or off-topic papers, and a regex sweep of all twenty for hell/hellfire/apostas*/ridda/eternal punishment/divine justice/theodicy/eternity returns ZERO hits. Any cluster page must therefore present this as a set of one man's arguments at a public lecture and in livestreams, not as a body of scholarship.</p><p>SEED TITLES EXCLUDED, AND WHY (read before adding any of them back). (1) 'Apostasy: Beyond the Rhetoric' (2018, sole byline, originally at islamicdiscourseinitiative.com) is EXCLUDED as evidence for this cluster and is listed here only with zero claims. Its full text was read: it is an essay about why Muslim youth are doubting and about online ex-Muslim polemicists (Hirsi Ali, Navabi, Rizvi, Namazie, Gaber), with a long digression on the Information Age and social media. It contains nothing on apostasy as a legal offence, nothing on punishment, nothing on hell and nothing on divine justice. A reader sent there for 'what does Islam say about apostasy' would be misled. The file's own provenance note also records it as a near-duplicate (token similarity 0.888) of the separately catalogued 'Naked Kings in the Information Age', so it must never be counted as a second piece of evidence. (2) 'When Religion Becomes Confusing' is NOT a case of personal religious difficulty - the archive holds it as _posts/2018-03-10-of-context-and-confusion.md, titled 'Of Context and Confusion', and it is a 478-word parable about a reader who judges a book from one quoted line. No afterlife, no divine justice, no apostasy. Excluded; it belongs to the quran-hermeneutics topic, where a maintainer should cross-link it. (3) 'An Alternate Reality' (2018) is a counterfactual geopolitical allegory about an Ottoman Empire that wins the world wars, ending on 'The world as we know it.' Its tags are terrorism/alternate reality. It is terrorism-cluster material, not divine-justice material. Excluded. (4) 'Two Islands' (2016) is an allegorical fable about colonialism in which two shipwrecked crews become the 'Civ'/'Sav' and codify Liberalism versus Submission. The only afterlife touch is a passing reference to 'the Deity who ruled over Paradise'. Excluded. (5) 'For Good Men to Do Evil' (2011) is a rebuttal of Steven Weinberg's rhetorical claim that religion makes good people do evil. It argues about human morality and religious exemplars; it never mentions hell, the afterlife or divine punishment. Excluded - and note the recovered file is _posts/2011-12-25-for-good-people-to-do-evil.md, so its slug says 'people' while the title says 'Men'.</p><p>THIS IS THE PRIMARY CLUSTER FOR 'My Views on the Punishment for Apostasy' - DO NOT DOUBLE COUNT. The terrorism-extremism cluster lists the same work with two quotes, for the narrow purpose of showing that the death-penalty question is discussed there. This cluster is its home. Cross-cluster reuse of the same quote is expected and fine; counting the same work in two cluster totals is not. That work is NOT in the seed list twice and there is no second apostasy essay in the archive.</p><p>A JURISTIC OPINION, NOT A SETTLED CONSENSUS - THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT THING A MAINTAINER MUST NOT GET WRONG. In the same paragraph in which he reports the consensus, he dissents from applying it: 'Having read scholarly works on the issue, it is the consensus that a Muslim who commits ridda must be killed. However, little else is discussed beyond that.' His position is conditional and he says so repeatedly: the punishment is part of the Shariah, the conditions that would warrant it are not met today because religious identity is no longer tied to the state, and the punishment would become operative again if that element were restored. The justification is his own reading of a reason that he says is 'largely absent from most explanations', built on an inference from Ibn Rushd's wording ('if taken captive before he declares war'). That inference is his; it is not a fatwa and the archive must not present it as one. He explicitly invites correction: 'I do wish to concede that I may be wrong on this matter and am perfectly okay with being corrected' and 'If my position is incorrect, I hope others will reach out to me to correct my view with sufficient evidence.' He also explicitly says many Muslims disagree with him, that there are Muslims he has had to argue with, and that he is 'among many Muslims who believe this' - so he is NOT claiming a majority, and neither may the cluster page. He reports on the record that scholars at Saudi and Medina universities 'still believe apostates should be killed', so a cluster page that presents his view as the scholarly consensus would be doubly wrong. His own gesture to the contrary is to send the reader onward: 'For a concise, but scholarly view on the subject (that may or may not support my view, but is educational nonetheless), please refer to Sh. Hatem al-Haj's article'. Reproduce that pointer and let the reader see that the author himself did not treat his position as the last word.</p><p>LEGAL RISK - A WARNING MUST BE ADDED BEFORE THIS CLUSTER PAGE SHIPS. Apostasy is criminalised by law, and in some jurisdictions by death, in a number of countries as of writing. The archive text names Malaysia, Pakistan, Bangladesh and others. The material here repeatedly discusses who is legally punishable for apostasy, what the constitutional position is, and whether apostates are imprisoned - a reader in one of those jurisdictions could reasonably read it as guidance. It is not legal advice and it is one man's argument. The cluster page should carry a standing warning on the same lines as the archive's existing no-contact position: nothing here is legal advice, the law of the reader's own country governs, and anyone who needs to know whether they are at risk should consult a qualified lawyer in that country rather than a 2020 blog post. Do not restate the '13 countries punish apostasy by death' figure anywhere: he is arguing against it in this very material ('It's probably closer to eight countries, which is still eight to many, but it's not 13'), and repeating it as a bare fact would both contradict the archive's source and put an inaccurate number in front of frightened readers.</p><p>MACHINE TRANSCRIPTS ARE NOT QUOTABLE AUTHORITY. Every transcript here carries the archive's own front-matter disclaimer: 'Transcript by machine - accuracy isn't perfect, so it shouldn't be used in polemics or debate material as an authoritative source.' Reproduce that disclaimer next to every transcript quote on the cluster page. Nine of the fourteen material items are transcripts. The auto-caption files also contain literal backslash-apostrophes and no punctuation, and the Whisper files contain recognisable mishearings that a reader WILL notice and that must not be silently corrected inside a quote: 'Alasabana' for Allah in the 'Is Allah a Dictator' transcript; 'fick'/'fake' for fiqh and 'tufar' for Tawfiq in the Muslim-vs-Ex-Muslim dialogue; 'health thing' for 'hell thing'; 'the turtle hellfire' for 'eternal hellfire' and 'a blaster' for 'eternity' in the Ohio State lecture; 'Glantan'/'Tanangano' for Kelantan and Terengganu and 'Nikiti Simbilan' for Negeri Sembilan in the Malaysia discussion; 'from art, theology' for what is almost certainly a reference to reason and theology; and 'will be fueled for the hell fire' where the sense is 'fuel for the Hell Fire'. Corrected readings belong in a note beside the quote, never inside it. Where a reader needs a clean, citable statement, prefer the two essays.</p><p>SPEAKER ATTRIBUTION IS NOT SAFE IN THE DIALOGUE TRANSCRIPTS. 'Muslim vs. Ex-Muslim' and '4 Lies Muslims Tell About Ex-Muslims' are two-speaker events and the transcripts carry no speaker labels. The determinism and genetics exchange in the Muslim-vs-Ex-Muslim dialogue, in which 'Allah is merciful enough that he will take those things into account' appears, is spoken by the other participant, NOT by the archive's subject - it is deliberately excluded from this spec. Any future quote taken from those two files must be checked by ear or by context before it is attributed. The same applies to '4 Lies': the claim that there are '13 countries in the world, all Islamic, which punish apostasy by death' is material he is reading out in order to debunk, not a claim he makes.</p><p>ONE STORY IS FLAGGED BY ITS OWN AUTHOR AS NOT CANONICAL. The Ohio State lecture contains the well-known account of three men taken into Hell, in which the man who looked back at God is let out first. On stage he says: 'there's a lovely story that my sheikh told me - I don't know if it's canonical or anything like this but I think it's derived from hadith but it's not, it's not exactly what it is it may have been just something that was passed down by scholars'. The claim is carried here because the author presents it and because the cluster's most-searched question ('who is it that God forgives?') turns on it, but the disclaimer must travel with it. Do not upgrade it to 'the Prophet said'.</p><p>WHAT A READER COULD MISTAKE FOR THE ARCHIVE'S OWN VIEW. The archive attributes; it does not endorse, and it is not a religious authority or a legal authority. Four things in particular need attribution on the same line as the claim. (1) 'The punishment for apostasy is part of the Shariah' is HIS statement that HE accepts the Shariah's content; a non-Muslim reader may hear it as the archive validating a legal code. (2) The claim that punishment 'is eternal because the offense is eternally committed' is a theological argument resting on reading Qur'an 6:27-28 and the 'arrogant' verses a certain way; it is contestable and the cluster page should say it is contestable, not merely that it is 'his view'. (3) The theological claims that evil has a divine purpose, that this-world suffering is rectified after death, and that 'our perception of good and evil is relative to us' are apologetic arguments, not findings, and the archive holds no counter-argument to set against them. (4) The named living people in this material - Saajid Lipham in the 2020 essay, and a Saudi sheikh and a YouTuber he calls the Apostate Prophet in the livestream - are named by him in order to be criticised. The cluster page must not present them as authorities or endorse his characterisation of them, and must not reproduce the he calls the Apostate Prophet video's claims as fact. Nothing in this cluster is an instruction to contact, approach or engage any of them; see the no-contact note below.</p><p>SOURCES THE CLUSTER PAGE SHOULD LINK BUT THIS SPEC DOES NOT QUOTE.
